What an Architectural Model Lets You See

At Jia Curated’s Architecture in Scale, Elok House shows how one maquette can make spatial relationships easier to inspect, while project documentation explains the intentions behind some of those decisions.

An architectural maquette is easy to admire as a miniature building. A more useful question is what it lets you see about the full-size one.

At Architecture in Scale, part of Jia Curated 2026, that shift in attention is worth making. Curated by Charmaine Chan, the exhibition brings together maquettes from 25 studios from Indonesia and wider Asia. Jia frames it as a way of encountering architecture beyond concepts and renderings.

Elok House, by CHANG Architects in Singapore, gives that idea a concrete form.

Look past the model as an object and its spatial organisation becomes the subject. Volumes stack and separate. Stairs connect levels. Voids interrupt the mass. Along one side, the building meets a difficult site edge created by a large retaining-wall condition.

Here, those elements are available to examine together.

The maquette does not, by itself, explain why the house is organised this way. CHANG Architects’ project documentation supplies that second layer.

Architect Chang Yong Ter has described Elok House as a response to the constraints of an intermediate-terrace site. He has explained that the retaining wall contributed to concerns around light and ventilation. The kitchen was moved towards the front of the house, while principal spaces were pulled away from the side boundaries to introduce more light and air.

With that context, the model can be read differently. The gaps between volumes, their position relative to the boundary and the circulation connecting them can be considered as parts of one spatial arrangement rather than isolated features.

That distinction matters.

The maquette does not establish ventilation effectiveness, daylight performance or ecological quality. Openings, voids and visible vegetation are not performance data.

What it does here is simpler: it gathers several parts of the design into one object where their relationships can be read together. The architect’s account then explains the intentions behind some of those decisions.

Elok House does not establish a rule for every project in Architecture in Scale. It is one example of what can happen when attention shifts from the miniature building itself to the relationships the miniature makes available for inspection.

That also means another form of representation does not need to lose for this one to be useful. The point is not whether a physical model is more truthful than a photograph, rendering or digital model. It is what this particular object makes available to examine.

Where do volumes meet or separate? How does circulation move between them? What has deliberately been left open? How does the building sit against the limits of its site?

Those questions provide a better way to approach the maquette than treating it simply as a small version of a finished building.

So when looking at an architectural model, ask:

What can I see together here that changes how I understand the design?
