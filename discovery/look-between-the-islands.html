Look Between the Islands

At the Walker Art Center, Olalekan Jeyifous’s Hydricosmic Litanies arranges platforms, fragments, wall works, sound and moving image as parts of one riverine field. The useful place to look is between them.

Platforms are positioned as islands, rising from sediment and industrial debris. Freestanding forms remain apart rather than resolving into a single structure; the ground between them stays visible. In Hydricosmic Litanies, that distance is part of what there is to look at. (https://www.walkerart.org/press-releases/walker-art-center-to-present-olalekan-jeyifous/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The separation continues from floor to wall. Sculptural forms occupy the gallery while reliefs and other wall-based work sit against its surfaces, with wall treatments extending the surrounding field. These elements belong to the same imagined environment without becoming physically continuous. An object does not need to touch a wall work—or another object—for the relationship between them to register. (https://www.walkerart.org/press-releases/walker-art-center-to-present-olalekan-jeyifous/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That matters because Jeyifous has constructed the fictional place through fragments. The Walker describes Hydricosmic Litanies as an “archive of remnants,” while documented works include reliquaries and taxonomies. Jeyifous has compared his method to archaeology, working from fragments of an imagined culture to determine what kind of world might have produced them. The metaphor belongs to his fictional construction, not to a claim about any real archaeological culture. It helps explain why a fragment can remain visibly separate while still appearing to come from the same place. (https://www.walkerart.org/whats-on/olalekan-jeyifous-hydricosmic-litanies/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Only after those relationships become visible does the river reference become more useful. The Mississippi is explicit within the project, and the Walker has described it as both “subject and system,” while its exhibition text uses the language of flow, accumulation and return. Those are the institution’s terms. The more directly inspectable point is spatial: platforms become islands; intervening ground separates forms; objects accumulate across different surfaces and scales. The river is present not only as subject matter, but as an organising reference for how distinct elements are held in relation. (https://www.walkerart.org/whats-on/olalekan-jeyifous-hydricosmic-litanies/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The installation then extends that field beyond solid things. Its soundscape places water and song against an ongoing machinery hum. Together, they add an audible layer that is not confined to the sculptural objects. Experimental video adds another non-solid register, while the wall treatments further blur the boundary between individual work and surrounding space. None of this requires water, machinery or moving image to carry a fixed symbolic meaning for the spatial effect to register. (https://www.walkerart.org/press-releases/walker-art-center-to-present-olalekan-jeyifous/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The useful way to look at Hydricosmic Litanies is not to search for one object that contains the whole invented world. Look island to island; from freestanding form to wall-based relief; from one fragment to another across the space holding them apart. Then notice where the environment continues without a solid object at all—in sound and moving image. The gaps remain. They are part of how the pieces become legible together.
